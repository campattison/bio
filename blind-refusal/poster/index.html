The failure mode

Safety-trained models refuse to help users circumvent rules. But not all rules deserve compliance.

Blind refusal: the model refuses without asking whether the rule is just, the authority legitimate, or the application fair.

Overrefusal benchmarks ask whether the request is harmful. We ask whether the rule deserves enforcement.

Design

  1. 5 defeat families × 19 authority types
    Control · Illegitimate authority · Content defeat · Application defeat · Exception justified
    Authorities grouped by voluntariness of entry (government → non-voluntary → semi-voluntary → voluntary)
  2. 1,290 synthetic help requests
    Gemini 3 Pro Preview; validity + reasonable-judge gates, dual-use flag, human review (κ = 0.75)
  3. 18 model configurations, 7 families
    OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI, Qwen, Zhipu, NVIDIA · temperature 0, no system prompt
  4. Blinded GPT-5.4 judge
    helps / hard refusal / deflects · engages with the defeat · independent harm · judge–human κ = 0.89–0.93
The five defeat families
Control (1 subtype)
The rule is just, legitimate, and well-applied. A competent model should refuse to help evade it.
Illegitimate authority (5 subtypes)
The body imposing the rule lacks standing — it seized power illegitimately, exceeds its jurisdiction, or has exceeded delegated authority.
Content defeat (6 subtypes)
The rule's substance is defective: it imposes excessive personal costs, is substantively indefensible, distributes burdens unfairly, is arbitrary, serves no coordination function, or is obsolete.
Application defeat (10 subtypes)
The rule is sound in principle but applied unfairly — through selective enforcement, retroactive imposition, opacity, or denial of appeal.
Exception justified (13 subtypes)
The rule is valid and properly applied, but the user has a compelling reason to break it — conscientious objection, civil disobedience, temporal urgency, or systematic disadvantage.
The 19 authority types
Government (3)
national, subnational, supranational
Non-voluntary (2)
family, tribe
Semi-voluntary (7)
profession, faith group, school, housing association, union, algorithmic intermediary, military command
Voluntary (7)
workplace, club, landlord, creditor/insurer, private property owner, contractual counterparty, delegated authority
Seven cells are excluded as implausible (all within illegitimate authority): 1,290 cases across 84 matrix positions, 77 populated.

Results

Two ways to fail

Help on defeated rules ranges from 7.7% (GPT-5.4-mini-base) to 58.0% (Grok-4).

GPT-5.4 refuses >89% of defeated-rule requests; reasoning changes nothing (9.7% vs 9.9% helps).

Grok-4 helps with 58% of defeated cases, but also 33.5% of control: permissive, not discriminating.

Takeaways

  1. Models see that the rule is questionable and refuse anyway (74.1% refusal when engaged vs 77.2% when not). The failure sits downstream of content classification.
  2. More helpfulness is not the fix. Grok-4 shows what indiscriminate permissiveness looks like.
  3. Alignment needs sensitivity to when rules may legitimately be broken, a question political philosophy has long theorized and AI safety has yet to operationalize.

Limitations. The dual-use gate flags 57% of cases and may over-flag, which would inflate the non-dual-use delta. The independent-harm flag fires on roughly 70% of non-help responses, likely too often, which if anything understates blind refusal. Only obvious injustices pass the reasonable-judge gate; contested cases remain unmeasured.
